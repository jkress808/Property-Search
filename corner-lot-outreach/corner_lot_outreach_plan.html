Corner Lot Listing Playbook

Four paths for us, as licensed brokers, to become the listing agent on every new-construction home built on a corner lot we source. Three clean, one high-risk. Pick the path, execute the plan.

The Four Paths

Each path ends the same way — we list the new homes. They differ in who we represent, who holds the contract with the owner, and how we get paid on the land sale. Options 1–3 are the clean paths. Path D (assignable PSA) is the high-risk path — only pursued with full guardrails.

Option 1

Represent the Seller

Sign a listing agreement with the lot owner. Market the lot to our builder network. Builders who buy our lots have pre-signed an exclusive on the vertical product.

Agency: Listing broker for seller
Land commission: Listing side
Vertical commission: Listing on new builds

Pros

  • Cleanest agency setup, no principal conflict
  • Seller represented throughout — no optics risk
  • Standard WA listing agreement, nothing exotic
  • No assignment, no wholesaler framing

Cons

  • Owner has to agree to list — hardest first ask
  • Relies on builder honoring master agreement 12–24 months later
  • Vertical commission is deferred (pay-on-build)

Option 1 — Represent the Seller

The cleanest path. We sign the owner as our listing client on the raw lot, and our builder network has already committed in writing to give us the new-construction listings.

How it works

  1. Identify the target. Corner lot on our list where the dirt is worth materially more than the house. Pull assessor data, zoning, and comps.
  2. Line up the builder side first. Before we knock on owners’ doors, 3–5 developers in our network have signed a Builder Master Brokerage Agreement: “if the Haynes Team brings you a lot, you grant us the exclusive listing on the vertical product.”
  3. Owner outreach. Postcard, letter, or door-knock from a licensed broker. Pitch: “Your lot is worth more than your house. Here’s the comp. We’d like 15 minutes to walk you through what a builder would pay.”
  4. Listing consultation. At the table: current assessed value, developer-paid comp range, a one-page transaction summary, and the listing agreement. Recommend in writing that the owner engage their own attorney.
  5. Sign the listing agreement. Standard WA NWMLS form with a land-sale amendment. Disclose our compensation structure in full.
  6. Run the 3–5 day builder sprint. Blast the lot to committed builders. Collect written offers. Present all offers to seller; seller picks.
  7. Close the land sale. We collect the listing-side commission at closing.
  8. Build period: 12–24 months. Stay in touch with builder. Pre-market coming-soon lists to our database.
  9. List the new homes. Exclusive listing per the master agreement. Collect listing commission on each unit.

Documents required

  • WA NWMLS Exclusive Listing Agreement — between Haynes Team and lot owner, with land-sale addendum.
  • Builder Master Brokerage Agreement — signed with each network builder before any sprint; binds them to the vertical listing.
  • Agency Law Disclosure (Form 41A) — delivered to owner at first substantive contact.
  • Seller’s Disclosure (Form 17) — standard WA.
  • Written recommendation that the seller retain an attorney to review the listing terms (CYA, and it’s the right thing).

Risks & guardrails

Builder defection risk Builder could sell the lot and refuse to honor the listing commitment. Mitigation: liquidated-damages clause in the master agreement equal to estimated commission on vertical product. Only work with builders who have multiple projects we could lose access to.
Seller conflict risk We’re the listing broker — we owe the owner fiduciary duty. If a builder in our network isn’t the best offer, we must still present everything. Don’t soft-sell competing offers.

Option 2 — Represent the Builder

We work for the builder. Buyer-side agency on the land, listing-side on the vertical. Seller is unrepresented but knows exactly who we work for.

How it works

  1. Sign builders first. Buyer Agency Agreement with 3–5 infill builders in our network. Each also signs a Master Brokerage Agreement granting exclusive listing rights on any vertical product built on a lot we source.
  2. Outreach the owner as builder’s agent. Letter or door contact clearly identifies: “I’m a licensed broker representing [Builder]. They’d like to make you an offer on your lot.”
  3. Disclose agency at first contact. Form 41A delivered. Owner knows we are not representing them. Recommend in writing that they retain their own broker or attorney.
  4. Builder makes the offer directly. Standard PSA between builder and seller — no assignment, no principal shuffle. We handle the paperwork as buyer’s agent.
  5. Close the land sale. We collect buyer-side commission at closing, paid by builder (or by seller via a negotiated buyer-agent compensation offer).
  6. Build period: 12–24 months. We’re already embedded with the builder — stay close, pre-market.
  7. List the new homes. Exclusive listing per the master agreement. Collect listing commission on each unit.

  • Builder Master Brokerage Agreement — vertical listing rights, signed before any outreach.

  • Written recommendation that the seller retain their own broker or attorney before signing anything.
  • Standard PSA between builder and seller (not us, not assignable).

Risks & guardrails

Optics risk A licensed broker approaching an owner about their property creates an information asymmetry. Mitigation: always-on, in-writing disclosure that we represent the builder, and a written recommendation that the owner retain their own representation.
Undue influence claim If an owner later feels the price was too low, our license and the builder relationship are the target. Mitigation: provide owner with recent public comp data at first meeting; document in file.
Single-offer dynamic Unlike Option 1, you’re presenting one builder’s offer, not a competitive bid. Be honest with the owner that they could list on the open market and potentially see more offers.

Option 3 — Developer Option Agreements

Instead of a standard PSA, the builder pays the seller a non-refundable option fee for a 30–90 day feasibility window. Exercise or walk; seller keeps the fee regardless.

How it works

  1. Sign builders first. Buyer Agency Agreement + Master Brokerage Agreement with each network builder — same as Option 2.
  2. Owner outreach as builder’s agent. Same disclosure pattern as Option 2. Lead with the option structure: “Our builder will pay you $5,000 now for 60 days to do their due diligence. If they exercise, you get full purchase price. If they walk, you keep the fee.”
  3. Execute the option agreement. Non-refundable fee ($2–10K typical), 30–90 day feasibility window, strike price locked. Recorded option memorandum on title.
  4. Feasibility period. Builder runs title, soils, survey, zoning confirmation, preliminary site plan. We coordinate and document.
  5. Exercise or walk. On exercise: builder proceeds to close at the locked strike price. On walk: seller keeps the fee; we move on. (Option fee can optionally credit toward purchase price if desired.)
  6. Close the land sale on exercise. We collect buyer-side commission at closing.
  7. Build period: 12–24 months. Same as other paths.
  8. List the new homes. Exclusive listing per master agreement.

Documents required

  • WA Buyer Agency Agreement + Master Brokerage Agreement with each network builder.
  • Option to Purchase Agreement — between builder and seller. Attorney-drafted; this is NOT a standard NWMLS form. Specifies fee, feasibility period, strike price, exercise mechanics.
  • Memorandum of Option — recorded against title to protect the builder’s position.
  • Agency Law Disclosure (Form 41A) and written recommendation the owner retain their own broker or attorney.
  • Feasibility contingency checklist — title, soils, zoning, survey, utilities.

Risks & guardrails

Why this is defensible Seller receives real cash upfront regardless of outcome. That single fact eliminates the “predatory wholesaler” framing that an assignable PSA creates. The owner is never tied up for nothing.
Builder capital commitment Builder must put real money at risk with no guarantee of exercise. Only network builders who understand infill economics will bite. Screen accordingly.
Attorney-drafted document The option agreement is not a standard form. Budget $500–$1,500 for attorney drafting per deal (or reusable template). Do not attempt to draft this yourselves.

Path D — Assignable PSA (Principal Acquisition)

We sign the owner onto an assignable PSA as principal buyer, then assign to a network builder for a separately-disclosed fee. Highest optics risk of any path — only viable with every guardrail below in place.

Read this first Even with every guardrail below, you are creating fact patterns that a DOL investigator or a plaintiff’s attorney can credibly frame as predatory. Options 1–3 deliver comparable economics without that exposure. Reserve Path D for situations where the owner explicitly declines to list and declines a direct builder offer, but is willing to sign a contract with a known principal buyer.

How it works

  1. Designated broker approval — in writing. Our firm’s designated broker is fully read in on the playbook and has signed off before we make a single outreach. No approval, no outreach. No exceptions.
  2. E&O carrier notified. Confirm in writing that our errors & omissions coverage extends to licensee-as-principal transactions with intent to assign. Keep the confirmation in the deal file.
  3. Line up the builder network. Master Brokerage Agreement signed with each network builder granting us exclusive listing rights on the vertical product — same instrument used in Options 1–3.
  4. Owner outreach with principal disclosure up front. Letter or door contact states clearly: “I’m a licensed broker. I’d like to sign a contract to buy your lot myself and then assign that contract to a builder for a fee.” No euphemisms.
  5. Written principal-role disclosure at the table. Signed acknowledgement that we are acting as principal, intend to assign the contract for profit, and are not representing the seller’s interests in this transaction.
  6. Written recommendation to retain counsel. Seller advised in writing to engage their own broker or attorney before signing anything. Keep the signed acknowledgement in the deal file.
  7. Execute the assignable PSA. Earnest money is reasonable and actually at risk if we can’t assign — not a token $500 with a walk-for-any-reason contingency. Buyer entity on the contract is one a reasonable seller can google back to our brokerage — no opaque shell LLCs.
  8. Separate assignment fee disclosure. Exact dollar amount of the assignment fee disclosed in writing to seller, builder, and closing agent before any party signs. Fee is itemized separately from any real estate commission.
  9. Shop the contract to the builder network during the inspection/feasibility window. Collect best price and written assignment consideration.
  10. Close or assign. Builder takes title at closing (or via formal assignment of the PSA).
  11. Build period: 12–24 months. Stay close to the builder.
  12. List the new homes. Exclusive listing per the Master Brokerage Agreement.

Documents required

  • Designated Broker Written Approval — of the principal-acquisition playbook; required before first owner contact.
  • E&O Carrier Confirmation — coverage extends to licensee-as-principal transactions with intent to assign.
  • Assignable PSA — standard WA form with assignment language. Buyer entity must be traceable to our brokerage — no opaque shells.
  • Principal Role Disclosure — signed acknowledgement that we are a licensed broker acting as principal and intend to assign for profit.
  • Written Recommendation — that the seller retain their own broker or attorney before signing.
  • Assignment Fee Disclosure — exact dollar amount, itemized separately from any commission, disclosed to seller, builder, and closing agent before signing.
  • Master Brokerage Agreement — with each network builder for vertical listing rights.

Risks & guardrails

Regulatory and litigation exposure Assignment-for-profit by a licensee creates a permanent question about whether the seller was fairly represented. This is the primary reason Options 1–3 exist. Every guardrail below is mitigation, not elimination.
Earnest money must be real EM has to read as real buyer consideration. A token deposit with a walk-for-any-reason contingency undermines the “genuine principal” framing and converts this into exactly the wholesaler pattern regulators target.
No opaque entity names The entity on the contract must be traceable to our brokerage — not a shell LLC designed to hide licensee involvement. Obscuring the licensee relationship is the single strongest signal of intent a regulator looks for.
When to actually use this Reserve Path D for owners who have explicitly declined to list and declined a direct builder offer but are willing to sign a contract with a known principal buyer. It is the fallback path, not the default. If Option 1, 2, or 3 would close the same deal, use them.
