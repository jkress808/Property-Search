00Overview

Why assessed value ≤ $600K matters for this list

  • Low assessed value + corner lot geometry = teardown/density play for a developer (townhomes, DADU + ADU, lot split).
  • Assessed value is a proxy for improvement value only; the dirt is the asset. Your pitch to the owner is about the land, not the house.
  • Your spread = (developer’s buy price) − (seller’s contract price) − (marketing + earnest money + title).

01Legal Framework (Washington State)

Wholesaling in Washington is legal but narrow. The two things that repeatedly get real-estate investors in trouble here are (a) acting as an unlicensed broker and (b) misrepresenting to a homeowner. Everything below is plain-English; have a WA real-estate attorney bless anything you actually mail.

Licensing line — RCW 18.85 Marketing a property you do not own or control by contract can be construed as acting as a broker and requires a license. The cleanest posture: you are marketing your equitable interest in a signed contract, not the property itself. You only market after the seller has signed.
Wholesaler disclosure trend Several states (including recent WA legislative activity around residential wholesaling) require a wholesaler to disclose in writing to the seller that: (i) you intend to assign the contract, (ii) you are not the end buyer, and (iii) the seller has the right to cancel within a stated window. Build this disclosure into your PSA whether or not a given bill is in force — it costs you nothing and kills the “you tricked me” lawsuit.
Equity Skimming Act — RCW 61.34 Applies when you deal with a distressed homeowner (default, pre-foreclosure). Penalties include treble damages. If any property on your list is in default, follow the Distressed Property Law (RCW 61.34) script specifically — not your general postcard.
Consumer Protection Act — RCW 19.86 Any unfair or deceptive practice in commerce can trigger attorney’s fees + treble damages. Translation: do not imply the property is worth less than it is, do not pressure-close, do not hide the assignment.

The clean posture (use this language with your attorney)

  • “I am a principal buyer. I buy properties as-is for cash. I may resell or assign my contract to another buyer at or before closing.”
  • Never: “I represent a developer.” That is brokerage language and triggers RCW 18.85.
  • Always deliver the final disclosure about assignment in writing, before signing, with a short cancellation window (3–5 business days).

APath A — Postcard Campaign

Lowest cost, lowest legal exposure, lowest conversion. Good for volume and building a “warm” pipeline. A postcard is marketing speech, not a contract — so the liability bar is low as long as the copy is truthful and doesn’t misrepresent you.

Cost / piece
$0.55–$0.90
Typical response
0.5%–2%
First-deal window
4–8 weeks

Execution steps

  1. Clean the list. From your corner-lot CSV, pull each owner’s mailing address (not just property address — many owners are landlords). King County Assessor’s Real Property Account data (the Real Property Account.zip file in this repo) has the taxpayer mailing address.
  2. Skip trace for absentee owners. Anyone whose taxpayer mailing address ≠ property address is absentee and typically a higher converter. Services: BatchSkipTracing, PropStream, REISkip (~$0.10–$0.25/hit for phone+mail append).
  3. Draft copy with three non-negotiable elements: (1) stated identity (your name or LLC), (2) stated intent (“I want to buy your property”), (3) contact method + opt-out.
  4. Print and mail. Vistaprint / Click2Mail / PostcardMania handle design, print, USPS presort. 6×9 or 6×11 jumbo has the best open rate for real estate.
  5. Track. Use a dedicated Google Voice or CallRail number, a unique landing page URL (e.g., a subdomain), and a unique email. This tells you which mailing triggered which lead.
  6. Screen leads. 3-question triage: motivation, timeline, price expectation. Kill anything that wants retail.
  7. Lock with a PSA. Once the lead is real, you move off the postcard track and onto Path B — the postcard’s only job is to generate a phone call.

Sample postcard copy (front)

We buy homes in [CITY] for cash, as-is. Hi [FIRST NAME or “Neighbor”], We’re local buyers interested in the property at [PROPERTY ADDRESS]. No repairs, no cleanout, no agent commission — we handle everything. If you’ve ever considered selling, we’d like to make you a no-obligation cash offer. Call or text: (206) XXX-XXXX [your-landing-page.com] — [Your Name / LLC] [Address, City, WA]

Sample postcard copy (back — compliance strip)

This is a marketing solicitation from [LLC], a principal buyer of real estate. We are not a licensed real estate broker and do not represent you. We may purchase and resell or assign our contract. To opt out of future mailings, call (206) XXX-XXXX or write to [LLC address].
Why the compliance strip matters It is the single cheapest liability reduction you will ever buy. It defeats “I thought you were my agent,” “I thought you were a broker representing a buyer,” and “I didn’t know you were going to resell.” Include it on every piece.

Liability notes for postcards

  • Low-risk channel. Postcards are marketing, not contracts. No one is bound by a postcard.
  • Don’t state a price. A price on a postcard can be construed as an offer and later weaponized (“your postcard said $400K and now you’re offering $350K”).
  • Don’t imply distress. “We know you’re behind on taxes” triggers RCW 61.34 analysis.
  • Don’t use stock photos of the actual house. Some carriers treat that as deceptive.
  • Honor opt-outs. Keep a suppression list. Scrubbing is 60 seconds and kills 90% of angry-owner risk.

BPath B — Mailed Contract (Blind Offer)

Aggressive. Higher-quality leads and shorter time-to-close, but the exposure is real: you are mailing a signable document. If the owner signs it and mails it back, you have a binding contract (or at least the appearance of one) — which means you can also be sued on it.

Cost / piece
$2.50–$5.00
Typical response
1%–3% (higher intent)
First-deal window
30–60 days

What’s in the mailed packet

  1. Cover letter. One page. Plain English. Explains who you are, why you’re writing, and exactly what’s in the envelope.
  2. Pre-filled Purchase & Sale Agreement (PSA) with these mandatory clauses:
    • Parties (you / your LLC as buyer, owner as seller)
    • Property description (legal + parcel #)
    • Purchase price (be specific — don’t “TBD”)
    • Earnest money amount + escrow holder (use a real title company, not your bank account)
    • Assignment clause: “Buyer may assign this agreement in whole or in part to any person or entity, and upon such assignment Buyer shall be released from further obligations.”
    • Inspection / feasibility contingency: 14–21 days. This is your escape hatch if you can’t find a developer assignee.
    • Closing date: 30–45 days from mutual acceptance.
    • As-is, no repairs, no seller financing.
    • Wholesaler / assignment disclosure (the plain-English paragraph in the Clarity section below).
    • Seller cancellation window: 3–5 business days after signing, for any reason.
  3. Disclosure sheet (plain English, large type, seller signs and keeps a copy).
  4. Self-addressed stamped envelope back to you or directly to the title/escrow company.
  5. “What happens next” one-pager walking through the 30-day close timeline.

Execution steps

  1. Price each offer. Do not send the same number to every owner. Pull land comps. Offer ~55–70% of ARV-of-land (what a developer pays per lot, minus your fee, minus contingencies).
  2. Have a WA attorney draft the PSA once. One drafting fee; reuse forever. Washington NWMLS forms are not designed for assignment-style wholesale — don’t use them off-the-shelf.
  3. Escrow first. Name the title/escrow company on the contract. This single detail signals legitimacy and also means signed contracts flow to a neutral third party, not your mailbox.
  4. Mail in tranches. 200–300 at a time so your phone can handle the inbound.
  5. Follow up by phone 5–7 days after mailing. The contract is an anchor; the phone call is what actually closes it.
  6. On signed return: open escrow, deposit EM, run title, begin marketing to developer list under a separate assignment agreement.
  7. Assign or terminate by feasibility deadline. If you can’t find an assignee inside the inspection period, terminate cleanly and refund EM per contract.

Sample cover letter

[Date] [Owner Name] [Mailing Address] RE: Cash Offer to Purchase — [Property Address], Parcel [PIN] Dear [Owner Name], My name is [Your Name]. I am a principal buyer of real estate in the Seattle area, buying through [LLC Name]. Enclosed is a written offer to purchase your property at [Property Address] for $[PRICE], cash, as-is, with a 30-day close through [Title Company]. A few things I want to be direct about: 1. I am not a real estate agent and I do not represent you. 2. My contract includes an assignment clause. That means I may assign my right to buy the property to another buyer (often a developer) before closing. You would still sell to whoever actually closes escrow, at the price on this contract. 3. You have 3 business days after signing to cancel, in writing, for any reason. 4. If you want to run this by an attorney, a family member, or an agent before signing, please do. Take your time. If the terms work for you, sign and return the enclosed documents in the postage-paid envelope to [Title Company]. If not, throw it away — there’s no obligation and I won’t follow up unless you ask. Sincerely, [Your Name], Managing Member [LLC Name] [Phone] · [Email]
Liability — this path has real edges
  • Binding-offer risk. A signed-and-returned PSA can be enforceable against you, not just the seller. If you can’t assign and can’t close, the seller can sue for specific performance or damages. The inspection contingency is what saves you — write it carefully.
  • Unauthorized practice risk. If your cover letter says you’re bringing a buyer for them, you’ve just acted as an unlicensed broker. Stay in first-person-principal voice the whole way through.
  • Elder / vulnerable-person risk. A blind written offer to an 85-year-old can be framed as predatory. Consider excluding owners > 75 or requiring a phone conversation before the contract is signed.
  • Earnest money abuse risk. $10 EM looks like a sham. Use real EM ($500–$2,500) held by a neutral title company.
  • Multi-owner risk. If the deed has two owners (spouses, siblings), both must sign. Sending a contract that only one signs and returns is a lawsuit waiting to happen.

COther Options Worth Considering

The postcard/contract binary isn’t the only shape this can take. Several of these lower your liability further or let you skip the licensing question entirely by structurally changing your role.

C1. Broker-partnered wholesale

  • Partner with a licensed WA real estate broker who signs as listing or dual-agent. You source the lead; the broker handles the paper and MLS compliance.
  • Your upside is smaller (you split the spread or pay a referral fee) but almost all RCW 18.85 risk disappears.
  • This is the single biggest liability reducer on this page. If you plan to do this at scale, build the broker partnership now.

C2. Bird-dog / direct referral to developer

  • You introduce the owner directly to a developer for a flat finder’s fee paid by the developer (not the seller).
  • No contract in your name, no assignment, no earnest money, no licensing question when done per-deal and not as a business.
  • Caveat: if it becomes your full-time activity, WA can treat it as unlicensed brokerage. One-off referrals are fine; a pipeline of referrals is a gray area — have your attorney draft the referral agreement.
  • Lowest liability, lowest per-deal upside. Great for your first 2–3 deals to build developer relationships.

C3. Option contract instead of PSA

  • Instead of a Purchase & Sale, sign an option: you pay $100–$500 for the right to buy at a set price inside 60–90 days.
  • Much cleaner to explain to the seller (“I’m paying you to hold the price”) and gives you runway to shop to developers.
  • If you don’t exercise, the seller keeps the option fee and walks. No breach-of-contract exposure.
  • Downside: some sellers balk because they feel tied up. Counter by offering a larger option fee ($1K+).

C4. Joint venture with a pre-committed developer

  • Line up the developer first. Sign a JV agreement: you source, they close, you split net profit.
  • You never hold title and never need to assign. The developer is the contract buyer from day one.
  • Eliminates the “what if I can’t find an assignee” risk completely.
  • Trade-off: you share upside, and you’re tied to one developer’s buy box.

C5. Door-knocking / in-person

  • Highest conversion for absentee-owned corner lots in a walkable zone.
  • Pair a postcard campaign with door-knocking 10–14 days later for anyone who didn’t call. Double touch > single touch.
  • Liability is moderate — the risk is verbal misrepresentation. Use a scripted opener and leave a printed leave-behind that says the same thing.
  • Not scalable past ~50 doors/week without a paid field rep.

C6. Ringless voicemail / SMS

  • Cheap and fast, but WA follows federal TCPA + has its own RCW 80.36.400 on commercial solicitation. Consent rules are strict and damages are $500–$1,500 per violation.
  • Not recommended unless you run it through a vetted compliance platform and only to owners who have opted in to your list.

C7. List-and-buy via your own licensed agent

  • If the owner is interested but wants “top dollar” and won’t take your direct offer, a licensed agent on your team can list the property on MLS and you (or a developer you’ve pre-lined-up) buy it through normal channels.
  • Lower margin but legally spotless. Useful for the 10–20% of leads that won’t take a below-market cash offer but are still motivated.

03Seller Clarity Rules

Every doubt an owner has about what just happened to them becomes a phone call to their attorney. The goal of this section is to make “I didn’t understand what I signed” unavailable as a defense.

The five things the seller must understand in writing

  1. Who you are. Legal name of buyer entity, natural person signing on behalf of it, and contact info.
  2. That you are the buyer, not their agent. You do not represent them. You are adverse to them in the sense any buyer is.
  3. That you may assign the contract to another buyer. And that the economic effect is: someone else may actually close. The seller gets paid either way, at the price on the contract.
  4. The price, the earnest money, and who holds it. No hand-wavy numbers.
  5. That they can cancel. Inside a stated window, in writing, for any reason, no fee.

Plain-English disclosure paragraph (include in every PSA packet)

PLAIN-LANGUAGE DISCLOSURE 1. The buyer in this contract is [LLC Name]. [LLC Name] is owned by [Name(s)]. We are real estate investors. We are not a licensed real estate broker and we do not represent you in this transaction. 2. This contract contains an assignment clause. This means we have the right to transfer our right to buy your property to another person or company, usually a developer or builder, before closing. The price you receive will not change. The person who closes and pays you may be someone other than us. 3. The earnest money on this contract is $[AMOUNT]. It will be held by [Title/Escrow Company] at [Address, Phone]. You can call them to confirm. 4. You have the right to cancel this contract for any reason within 5 business days after signing it, by sending us written notice at [Email / Address]. If you cancel in that window, the contract is void and you owe us nothing. 5. You are allowed — and encouraged — to have this contract reviewed by an attorney, an accountant, a real estate agent, or a trusted family member before you sign. We will wait. I understand the above. _________________________ _________________________ Seller signature / date Seller signature / date
Why this specific structure This disclosure tracks the pattern used by states that have already enacted wholesaler-transparency statutes (VA, IL, OK) and is defensible under WA’s Consumer Protection Act. A plaintiff’s attorney needs a “deceptive act” to bring a claim; signing this paragraph is an affirmative record that no deception occurred.

04Liability Guardrails

Concrete things to do, and concrete things never to do, to keep your exposure bounded.

Always do

  • Transact through an LLC, not personally. Keep it in good standing with the WA Secretary of State.
  • Carry general liability + E&O on the LLC if you can get it at this volume.
  • Use a neutral title/escrow company for every earnest money deposit.
  • Include a written assignment disclosure in every PSA.
  • Give a 3–5 day seller cancellation window in writing.
  • Keep a suppression / do-not-contact list and scrub every mailing.
  • Keep copies of every piece of mail you send, with date and list used. Cheap insurance.
  • Have a WA real estate attorney on retainer (flat monthly, ~$500–$1,500). One saved claim pays for a year.
  • Run title early, not at close. Broken chain of title is the #1 reason a wholesale deal dies.

Never do

  • Never say you “represent a buyer” or a developer. That is brokerage language.
  • Never market the property publicly before you have a signed contract. You don’t own it — marketing it is brokering it.
  • Never use sham earnest money ($1, $10). Doesn’t look like a real contract; courts notice.
  • Never target distressed / pre-foreclosure owners on your general list. Separate pipeline, separate script, RCW 61.34 compliance.
  • Never send a contract to an obviously incapacitated owner (e.g., guardianship on file). Pull that name from the list.
  • Never inflate your marketing of the deal to your developer list beyond what the contract allows — no “no-contingency, guaranteed close” if your PSA has a feasibility out.
  • Never amend the contract after signing without the seller’s signature on the amendment. Forgery allegations end careers.
Recommended insurance / structural stack LLC (WA formation) → operating agreement that permits assignment → E&O + general liability ($1M/$2M) → title/escrow relationship with a local company (CW Title, Chicago Title, First American) → attorney on retainer → CRM + mailing suppression list.

05Recommended Path

Given your stated constraints (team liability + seller clarity), here’s the sequencing we’d actually run.

Step 1 — Line up the back end first (1–2 weeks)

  • Build a developer buyer list of 8–15 active Seattle/Shoreline infill developers. Know each one’s buy box: min/max lot size, price range, zoning preference.
  • Sign a short JV or buyer-intent letter with 2–3 of them. Not exclusive — just a “we’ll close if the deal fits” handshake on paper.
  • Open a title/escrow relationship now, before you mail anything.
  • Have a WA RE attorney draft your PSA, assignment agreement, and disclosure paragraph. One-time cost.

Step 2 — Start with Path A at volume (weeks 2–4)

  • Mail a postcard to the full corner-lot list. Cost: likely under $1,500 for a 2,000-name list.
  • Second touch 3 weeks later to non-responders.
  • Every inbound lead gets a phone screen, and promising ones move to Step 3.

Step 3 — Use Path B surgically on warm leads

  • Do not blanket-mail the PSA. Only send the contract to owners who have talked to you and verbally agreed on a price.
  • This is a meaningful liability reduction: the contract no longer arrives cold. You’ve already confirmed identity, capacity, and price.
  • Every PSA goes out with the plain-English disclosure + 5-day cancellation paragraph.

Step 4 — Reserve Path C options for specific leads

  • Absentee owners who can’t decide → option contract (C3).
  • Owners who want retail pricing → list via partner broker (C1 / C7).
  • Slam-dunk lots where a developer is already eager → bird-dog referral (C2) or JV (C4), skip the contract entirely.
Why this sequence beats postcard-only or contract-only Postcard-only is slow and non-binding. Contract-only is legally loud and mails a signable document to strangers. The hybrid — postcard to open the door, PSA only after a phone screen — gets you Path B’s close rate with something close to Path A’s liability footprint.

06Pre-Launch Checklist

Do not mail a single piece until every item below is checked.

  • LLC formed in WA and in good standing
  • Operating agreement permits assignment of real estate contracts
  • EIN obtained; LLC bank account open
  • WA real estate attorney engaged; PSA + disclosure + assignment agreement drafted
  • Title / escrow company relationship opened
  • Dedicated phone number (CallRail / Google Voice) + landing page live
  • Dedicated email address for this campaign
  • CRM set up to track mailings, inbound calls, suppression list
  • Developer buyer list compiled with buy-box criteria per developer
  • 2–3 soft handshake JV / buyer-intent letters in place
  • Corner-lot CSV cleaned; taxpayer mailing address appended; absentee flag set
  • Skip-trace run on absentee owners
  • Elderly / vulnerable / guardianship entries flagged and reviewed
  • Distressed-property entries (tax delinquency, pre-foreclosure) moved to a separate pipeline
  • Postcard design + compliance strip approved by attorney
  • Suppression list file created (for opt-outs and DNC)
